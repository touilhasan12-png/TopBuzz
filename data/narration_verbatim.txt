--- COLD OPEN ---

On Saturday, October 17th, Iran's Revolutionary Guards Navy announced
that the Strait of Hormuz is closed, and that passage is prohibited.
That's according to Iran's state news agency, IRNA.

The day before, the head of US Central Command had said something quite
different. In a video message, Admiral Brad Cooper said the strait is
"controlled by the United States and our regional partners, not Iran."
He said about four hundred merchant ships had crossed in the previous
seven days.

One narrow waterway. Two official statements.

And then there are the independent trackers, which are counting something
else again: anywhere from single digits to a dozen or so ships a day. On
October 18th, the maritime-intelligence firm Windward counted twelve,
four of them with their tracking signals off.

So how can a strait be closed and open at the same time?

Here's the short answer. "Closed" isn't one thing. There's closed by law.
Closed by weapons. Closed by insurance. Closed by permission. Closed by
fear. And those don't always move together.

This video is not about who is right. It's about how it works: the
mechanisms that decide whether a strait is open, closed, or somewhere in
between.

One rule before we start. Every number you'll hear is attributed to a
named source, and all of them are listed in the description. Where
sources disagree, you'll hear both.


--- PART 1: THE STAKES ---

The Strait of Hormuz connects the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman. At its
narrowest, it's only about 35 to 40 kilometers wide.

The International Energy Agency says about a quarter of the world's
seaborne oil trade, and about 20 percent of its liquefied natural gas,
moved through it in 2025. Before the war began on February 28th, the
agency puts the oil flow at around 20 million barrels a day.

The IEA calls what followed the largest supply disruption in the history
of the global oil market. In its October report, published on October
14th, the agency estimated that oil flows through the strait recovered in
September to about 10.9 million barrels a day. That's up by 3.7 million
from August, but still only a little over half of the pre-war level.

That is why the story matters far beyond the Gulf: fuel and shipping
costs everywhere are exposed to it.


--- PART 2: TWO STATEMENTS ---

Let's start with exactly what each side is saying.

Iran. The Revolutionary Guards Navy statement, carried by IRNA, said the
strait is closed and passage is prohibited. It warned ships against
violating Iranian regulations. The Guards' spokesman, Mohebbi, has said
that an open strait should see about 125 ships and 20 million barrels a
day passing through. Those levels are not being met, he says, so the strait
is closed.

This was not the first such announcement. The Joint Maritime Information
Center, known as JMIC, which is run by the 47-nation Combined Maritime
Forces, recorded an Iranian proclamation that the strait was closed on
July 12th as well. Its advisories include figures supplied by US forces.
The Guards themselves described the October 17th statement as another
declaration.

The United States. On October 17th, Captain Tim Hawkins, a spokesman for
US Central Command, said the strait is open and available for transit,
and that about 97 percent of the ships that sought to cross have done so
successfully. The day before, CENTCOM's commander, Admiral Brad Cooper,
said the strait is controlled by the United States and its regional
partners, not Iran. He said about 400 merchant ships had crossed in the
previous seven days, and that US forces had helped Gulf partners ship more
than 100 million barrels of crude in that same week.

Two details about the reporting. In the coverage used here, the 97 percent
figure came without a time window or a ship count, and Cooper's seven days
were not broken down day by day. And the Guards' 125-ship threshold is their own
definition of "open."

Now, one way to read all this. It's this video's reading, not either
side's. Iran is describing "open" as normal: normal traffic, normal
volumes, no interference. The US is describing "open" as ships getting
through. Part of the gap is definitional. But the statements also make
competing claims about who controls the strait, and that reading doesn't
settle those.


--- PART 3: THE COUNTING PROBLEM ---

So what's actually happening on the water? This is where it gets
complicated, because the numbers themselves disagree.

First: what's normal? It depends on the dataset. The International
Monetary Fund's PortWatch, as cited by the economics publication
EconoFact, showed an average of about 103 trading-ship arrivals a day in
the week before the war. Reuters reports
about 125 large commercial vessels a day. gCaptain cites a 2025 average
of about 138. And some tracker sites cite figures closer to 85, depending
on which ships they count.

Second: what's happening now? Kpler counted seven commodity vessels on
October 6th, not counting ships with their transponders off. Reuters
reported that as the lowest since July 23rd. In its October 15th advisory,
marked as a draft, JMIC reported single-digit daily numbers for October
8th to 14th, from independent data on cargo ships and tankers that
excludes vessels calling at nearby Iranian ports. And Windward's own page
showed twelve crossings on October 18th, five inbound and seven outbound,
four of them with their transponders off. Windward also cautions that
counts of dark transits can't always be compared across dates, because
its detection coverage has changed.

Third: the US count. The same advisory cites US Navy shipping-guidance
data showing 121 US-facilitated transits between October 11th and 14th.
That's about 30 a day.

Then comes one of the most detailed documents available: the weekly
overview from UKMTO, the UK Maritime Trade Operations center, which
describes itself as a neutral and impartial information provider. In its
report dated October 16th, covering data through October 14th, UKMTO
recorded 254 combined transits, about 70 percent below pre-war levels. But
look inside that number. Only 42 were observed through AIS, the
ship-tracking signal. UKMTO adds that the US-stated facilitated transits
are a separate dataset, which it says it can't independently verify. It
calls the two sets "related but distinct."

So why can't anyone simply count? Because of AIS. Ships are supposed to
broadcast their position. But in this part of the world, many don't, and
JMIC reports that ships broadcasting AIS face a higher risk of being
targeted. Other signals are distorted: UKMTO logged more than 23,000
anomalous AIS positions in a single week, in areas where it also received
reports of satellite-navigation interference. This video doesn't attribute
that to anyone.
In one snapshot on May 8th, Windward detected 521 radio transmissions
around Hormuz, and only 84 matched an AIS broadcast.

In early September, Drewry analyst Eirik Hooper told Al Jazeera that a US
operational count "plausibly includes" everything that moved under or near
naval protection, including support ships, tugs and small coastal boats.
That is his inference, a hypothesis, not a finding, and none of the other
sources used in this video confirms it.

So here's the honest takeaway. When you see a number about Hormuz, ask
three questions. Which ships are counted? Over what window? And how does
the source know?


--- PART 4: SEVEN LEVERS ---

A strait isn't a door. It doesn't close with one handle. This video sorts
the mechanisms into seven levers. Some sit with governments, and some
don't.

LEVER ONE: LAW AND DECLARATION.

Under the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea, ships have a right of
"transit passage" through straits like Hormuz, and coastal states may not
suspend it. Iran has not ratified that convention, and neither has the
United States. Reuters reports that experts generally
treat the core rules as customary international law, while Iran argues it
has consistently objected to them. In a July advisory, JMIC said the
strait is not subject to coercion or control by any single nation. Iran says
its restrictions are national security and self-defense measures in
wartime.

A declaration, by itself, doesn't stop a single ship. What it does is
change what happens if one tries.

LEVER TWO: WEAPONS.

In this video's judgment, this is the lever with the most immediate
effect. UKMTO recorded 12 attacks
in its latest weekly report, ten of them in the strait. JMIC counts 149
confirmed vessel incidents across the wider region since March 1st, and
rates the threat level in the strait as "severe", the second-highest on
its scale. The International Maritime Organization's list of highlighted
incidents shows 24 seafarer deaths as of October 14th.

Who is responsible is often left unstated in the reports themselves.
UKMTO's incident entries mostly say "unknown projectile." CENTCOM says the
container ship GFS Pride was hit by a projectile from Iran's Revolutionary
Guards. The Guards said it struck a mine, on a route Iran says was
unauthorized. JMIC said there was no substantiation for the mine
claim.

One more detail: UKMTO says 55 of the 74 projectile strikes since July 6th
hit ships on the southern route, the lane where US-assisted transits take
place. This video draws no conclusion from that.

LEVER THREE: MINES.

A mine doesn't need to hit anything to close a strait. The suspicion is
enough. In 1987, the supertanker Bridgeton struck a mine during the first
US-escorted convoy of Operation Earnest Will.

In late August, Admiral Cooper said US forces had cleared the main
shipping lanes of mines. Bloomberg reported that some allies privately
doubted it. JMIC's advisories continue to warn of possible mining in and
around the traditional shipping lanes.

On October 17th, Iran's Guards said two tankers hit mines after trying a
route Iran hadn't authorized. State media put the cargo at about two
million barrels of crude. Reuters noted there was no independent
confirmation.

It wasn't the first such claim. At the end of August, Iran's Guards said a
large tanker on the southern route had hit two mines. US Central Command
answered that no ship was struck by mines and called the claim false.
Neither side offered evidence in the reporting used here.

So here is what's on the record. Iran says mines. The US says the main lanes are
clear. And nobody independent has confirmed the October 17th incidents.

LEVER FOUR: INSURANCE.

Ships can't sail without it. Early in the war, the Lloyd's Market
Association pushed back on the idea that cover had disappeared. War
insurance remained available, it said. In its view, ships were held back
because masters and owners judged the risk to crew and vessel too high.

But the price moved. Before the war, war-risk premiums were around a
quarter of one percent of a ship's value, according to The National. By
July, a Marsh executive told S&P Global that additional war-risk premiums
had reached 7.5 to 10 percent of hull value. On a 100-million-dollar
tanker, that works out to between 7.5 and 10 million dollars. Those are
July figures. Rates are repriced constantly, so
treat them as a snapshot, not today's price.

LEVER FIVE: PERMITS AND FEES.

Since May, Iran has run a permit system through a body it calls the
Persian Gulf Strait Authority. Ships submit an application by email and
are directed to a route near Iran's coast. In March, an Iranian lawmaker
said on state television that some ships were being charged two million
dollars. The sources used here carry no independent confirmation of that
figure.

The US Treasury sanctioned that authority on May 27th, called its tolls
illegitimate, and warned that anyone cooperating could face sanctions
risk. In July, President Trump announced that the US would charge a
20 percent fee on cargo passing through the strait, though the sources
used here give no details on how it would work. And JMIC, in a July
advisory, said there is no controlling authority regulating passage and no
fee required for any route.

So a shipowner faces two rival sets of rules, and a different kind of
risk with each.

LEVER SIX: VISIBILITY.

This one is almost invisible. According to JMIC, ships that broadcast AIS
face a higher risk of being targeted, and are hailed with instructions to
divert to Iran's northern route. Meanwhile, the Guards' naval commander
said this week that vessels that deactivate their navigation systems
would be considered hostile.

Two different warnings. Ships face risk whether they are seen or not.

LEVER SEVEN: NAVAL FACILITATION.

The US doesn't describe most of this as classic escorts. The words are
"assisted" and "coordinated protection." Ships can get route guidance from
a US Navy cell, and according to JMIC, that coordination is voluntary.
Most of it runs along a southern route hugging the coast of Oman.

At the same time, CENTCOM describes a US blockade of ships linked to
Iranian ports. JMIC, citing CENTCOM figures, says 135 vessels had been
redirected and four disabled as of October 10th and 11th.

So both sides run a control system. Iran runs permission on the northern
lane. The US runs guidance on the southern lane, plus what CENTCOM
describes as a blockade. UKMTO says most ships broadcasting AIS still use
Iran's route. It also cites a ratio of about five to one, which its report
doesn't fully explain.

The strait is effectively two lanes with two rulebooks.


--- PART 5: SHIPS VERSUS BARRELS ---

Here's a wrinkle that explains some of the confusion. Ship counts are low.
But oil volumes aren't as low as ship counts would suggest.

The IEA says September's increase, to about 10.9 million barrels a day,
was helped by robust ship-to-ship transfers in the Gulf of Oman, where
cargo moves from one tanker to another outside the strait. It also reports
that pipelines bypassing the strait raised bypass exports to 5.6 million
barrels a day. And Kpler told AFP that most crude crossing the strait
changes tankers offshore.

And daily numbers swing wildly. Kpler data cited by Al Jazeera shows
nearly 20.8 million barrels crossing on October 8th, and only 2.9 million
on October 11th. CENTCOM, for its part, said US forces helped Gulf
partners ship more than 19 million barrels on Monday, October 12th. Those
numbers use different definitions and different time windows, which is why
weekly averages are more reliable than any single day.

That is one reading of why low ship counts and large barrel numbers can
appear in the same news cycle.


--- PART 6: SO WHAT DOES "CLOSED" MEAN? ---

Let's go back to the question. There are three ways to be closed.

Legally closed. Does anyone have the authority to shut it? That's
disputed. JMIC said in July that no single nation controls the strait.
Iran says it can, as a matter of security.

Physically closed. Can a ship get through? Yes. Some do, every day:
roughly 30 a day by the US count, a dozen or fewer by independent
trackers. None of the sources used here reports zero.

Commercially closed. Will ordinary shipowners send ordinary cargoes at
ordinary cost? On that test, UKMTO puts traffic about 70 percent below
pre-war levels. JMIC's three anchorage counts add up to roughly 430 ships at anchor
off the UAE and Oman. And by the IEA's figures, September's oil flows were
a little over half of the pre-war level.

In this video's reading, Iran's statement leans on that third definition,
and on a claim to authority. The US statement leans on the second, and on
its ability to help ships through. They also make competing claims about
who controls the strait.

A strait doesn't close like a door. It closes like a market: through fear,
price and permission. And right now, neither side's declaration alone
controls all three.

What should you watch? The weekly UKMTO overview, published on Fridays.
JMIC's threat level. War-risk insurance quotes. The IEA's monthly report.
And diplomacy. On October 8th, President Trump said on social media that
the US would not attack Iran before the November 3rd midterm elections,
and that his team was having productive discussions with Iran. Iran's
foreign minister said Tehran expected to give a formal reply to a US
counterproposal within days. As of October 18th, no agreement had been
announced in the sources used here.

Everything in this video reflects information available as of October
18th, 2026. This situation is changing day by day. Sources for every
figure are in the description.
