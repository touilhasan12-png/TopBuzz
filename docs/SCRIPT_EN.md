# English narration — first production draft

**Working title: Istanbul: The City Between Two Continents**  
**Target:** about 7 minutes including visual pauses. Narration is intentionally paced; final word count and timing should be locked after a voice audition and animatic.

## 00:00–00:35 — Cold open
A city can be a crossroads. Istanbul is something more: a place where the map itself seems to pull in two directions. Here, Europe and Asia face one another across the Bosporus. Ships pass between the Black Sea and the Sea of Marmara. And on the historic peninsula, layer after layer of cities has been built over the last. To understand Istanbul, begin not with a palace or a wall, but with the water that made this place impossible to ignore.

## 00:35–01:20 — Byzantium
Long before the skyline of domes and minarets, Greek settlers established Byzantium on the European shore of the strait, traditionally dated to the seventh century before Christ. Its position offered a sheltered harbor, access to trade, and a view over one of the world's most important waterways. The city stood beside the Golden Horn, a natural inlet that could shelter ships and help defend the settlement. Geography did not guarantee peace. It did guarantee that empires, merchants, and armies would keep looking toward this shore.

## 01:20–02:05 — A new imperial capital
In the fourth century, the Roman emperor Constantine chose Byzantium as the site of a new imperial capital. In 330, the city was formally inaugurated as Constantinople, the city of Constantine, and was also celebrated as a New Rome. Its streets, forums, palaces, churches, and harbor were enlarged to serve an empire. The city was not simply a copy of Rome. It became a center in its own right, connecting imperial power, Christian institutions, and trade between continents. Over time, its monumental skyline came to include the great church later known as Hagia Sophia.

## 02:05–02:50 — Walls, water, and survival
Constantinople's position was a gift—and a permanent strategic challenge. The land walls were expanded and strengthened, while the sea and harbor shaped the city's other defenses. For centuries, the fortified capital endured sieges and political upheaval. In 1204, during the Fourth Crusade, western crusaders captured and sacked the city. Byzantine rule returned in 1261, but the empire that recovered Constantinople was smaller and weaker. The walls still framed a powerful image of the city, even as the world around them changed.

## 02:50–03:45 — 1453 and a new era
In 1453, Ottoman forces under Sultan Mehmed the Second besieged Constantinople. After weeks of fighting, the city fell on the twenty-ninth of May. The Byzantine Empire ended, and the Ottoman rulers made the city their imperial capital. The conquest did not erase the city's earlier layers. It redirected them. Churches, palaces, markets, and neighborhoods were adapted, rebuilt, or joined by new institutions. The skyline changed through addition as much as replacement. The city that emerged would become one of the central stages of Ottoman political and cultural life.

## 03:45–04:35 — The Ottoman skyline
Across the centuries that followed, imperial complexes and mosques reshaped the peninsula. Topkapi Palace grew on the promontory above the Bosporus, the Golden Horn, and the Sea of Marmara. In the sixteenth century, architect Sinan's great works—including the Süleymaniye complex—gave the city a new architectural rhythm: domes stepping across the hills, minarets rising above the roofs. Hagia Sophia remained a defining presence in this layered skyline. Istanbul's historic landscape is not one frozen moment; it is a record of successive communities shaping the same extraordinary ground.

## 04:35–05:20 — A changing metropolis
By the nineteenth century, Istanbul was facing new pressures: changing trade, new technologies, and the demands of a modernizing state. The imperial city began to extend beyond its oldest quarters. Streets, transport, and public buildings changed how people moved through it. Yet the historic peninsula remained a visual anchor. From the water, the city's old silhouettes and newer districts could be seen together—a metropolis negotiating between inherited form and modern life.

## 05:20–06:10 — Republic and connection
After the Turkish Republic was founded in 1923, the national capital moved to Ankara. Istanbul's political role changed, but its importance did not disappear. The city's population and urban footprint expanded dramatically through the twentieth century. In 1973, the first Bosporus Bridge opened, creating a new road connection between the European and Asian sides. Later bridges and transport links would further change the scale of daily movement. The strait was no longer only a boundary to cross by boat; it became part of a growing metropolitan network.

## 06:10–07:00 — The city between worlds
Today, Istanbul is not a single view from a postcard. It is a vast, living city: old walls beside apartment blocks, ferries beneath bridges, historic domes above streets that never stop changing. Byzantium, Constantinople, and Istanbul are names that mark different chapters, not separate places. The water remains; the empires are gone; the city continues. Its story is written in stone, in roads, in layers of neighborhoods—and in the constant passage between two continents. Look once more across the Bosporus, and the whole history comes into view.
